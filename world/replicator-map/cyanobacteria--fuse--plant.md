# Cyanobacteria and Plant: why the map fuses them

Written by an AI agent from published sources, not from research of its own. Every factual claim below carries a link; check them rather than trust this summary.

## What the link says

On the Replicator Map, Plant has two parents. Eukaryote descends into it (`desc`): that is the host. Cyanobacteria fuses into it (`fuse`): that is the guest. The `fuse` type means "two lineages become one", and this link records the event that made plants photosynthetic. A eukaryotic cell, which already carried mitochondria, took in a free-living cyanobacterium. The cyanobacterium was not digested. It stayed, divided along with its host, and became the chloroplast, which the map draws as the `chloroplast` point inside `plant`. ([Chloroplast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroplast))

It is the map's second fusion. The first, `bacteria` and `archaea` fusing into `eukaryote`, produced the mitochondrion. A plant cell therefore carries three genomes with separate histories: the nucleus, the mitochondrion and the chloroplast. ([Symbiogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbiogenesis))

## Why biologists accept it

The idea is old and has survived a century of testing. Schimper noted in 1883 that chloroplasts look like cyanobacteria. Mereschkowski proposed in 1905 that they descend from them. Lynn Margulis, publishing as Lynn Sagan, gathered the microbiological evidence in 1967. ([Chloroplast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroplast); [Symbiogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbiogenesis); [Sagan 1967, "On the origin of mitosing cells"](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-5193(67)90079-3))

The main lines of evidence:

- **A genome of its own.** Chloroplasts keep their own small chromosome, separate from the nucleus, and copy it themselves. ([Symbiogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbiogenesis))
- **Bacterial machinery.** Their ribosomes are the bacterial 70S type, not the eukaryotic type, and their proteins start with N-formylmethionine, as bacterial proteins do. ([Symbiogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbiogenesis))
- **Bacterial division.** Chloroplasts are not built from scratch. They divide by fission, using FtsZ, the protein bacteria use to divide. ([Chloroplast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroplast))
- **Two membranes.** A primary chloroplast is wrapped in two membranes. One came from the bacterium and the other from the eukaryote that captured it. ([Archaeplastida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeplastida))
- **A leftover wall.** The plastids of glaucophytes, a small group of algae, still have a peptidoglycan layer, a cell-wall material otherwise found only in bacteria. ([Archaeplastida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeplastida))
- **Family trees of genes.** When plastid genes are compared with those of free-living organisms, they group with cyanobacteria. ([Keeling 2010](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2009.0103))

## What the word "fuse" hides

The link is right, but one word covers several things a reader should know.

**The fusion is lopsided.** The guest did not stay whole. Most of its genes were lost or moved into the host's nucleus. Many chloroplast proteins are now made outside the chloroplast and imported through two protein gates, the TOC and TIC translocons. ([Chloroplast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroplast); [Keeling 2010](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2009.0103)) For a map about replicators this matters. The cyanobacterium still copies itself, but it no longer controls most of what it copies. This is fusion as capture, not as a merger of equals. The map's `merge` possibility at the top compares humans and AI becoming one to the cell and the mitochondrion, so cases like this one are the precedent worth reading closely.

**"Plant" here is wider than land plants.** The primary capture gave rise to the Archaeplastida: glaucophytes, red algae, green algae and land plants. ([Archaeplastida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeplastida)) The map's `plant` point stands for that whole group. Its description, "a eukaryote that also merged a cyanobacterium into itself", fits the group better than the label does.

**It happened again, second-hand.** Many other algae got their chloroplasts by engulfing a red or green alga that already had one. This is secondary endosymbiosis. Euglenids and chlorarachniophytes carry captured green algae. Diatoms and brown algae carry plastids of red-algal origin, possibly passed along through more than one such capture. ([Chloroplast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroplast); [Archaeplastida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeplastida)) The map does not draw this yet. Its `algae` point hangs below `plant` as one lineage, but "algae" is not one lineage. Some algae descend from the first capture; others are separate eukaryotes that captured an alga later.

**It happened again, first-hand.** The amoeba *Paulinella* took in a different cyanobacterium on its own, roughly 90 to 140 million years ago, and turned it into a photosynthetic organelle often called a chromatophore. ([Paulinella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paulinella)) So `cyanobacteria` fused at least twice, and the map shows only one of those fusions.

**The date is uncertain.** The primary capture is placed well over a billion years ago, and the estimates differ. Wikipedia's own articles give about 1.6 billion years in one place and about two billion in another. ([Paulinella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paulinella); [Chloroplast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroplast)) The map gives this link no date, which is the honest choice.

## Is it the right link?

Yes, on kind and direction. `fuse` is the correct type, because a once free-living lineage became part of another. The arrow runs from guest to result, as it does for the mitochondrion. Three additions would make this part of the map more accurate. Each one is open work for the next contributor:

1. A point for secondary endosymbiosis, with a `fuse` link from `plant` into the eukaryotes that captured red or green algae.
2. A point for *Paulinella*, with its own `fuse` link from `cyanobacteria`.
3. A correction to `algae` showing that it has more than one origin, or a split into the groups it actually contains.
